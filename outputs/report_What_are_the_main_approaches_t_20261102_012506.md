## Executive Summary

The transition of artificial intelligence from static text synthesis to autonomous decision-making hinges on building agents that can reason, plan, and execute actions within dynamic environments. This review synthesizes literature addressing the core question: what are the main approaches to building AI agents that can reason and act? 

The synthesized research reveals four primary architectural paradigms: (1) synchronous interleaving of reasoning traces with external tool invocations to ground action spaces; (2) deliberative tree-search planning that overcomes the constraints of linear autoregressive generation via heuristic exploration, lookahead, and backtracking; (3) non-parametric behavioral adaptation driven by episodic verbal self-reflection and structured memory streams; and (4) multi-agent conversational frameworks that distribute complex problem-solving across specialized, interacting entities. 

A central caveat emerges across this literature: while language model agents exhibit strong heuristic flexibility, their underlying reliance on statistical pattern matching rather than first-principles world models leaves them vulnerable to degenerative action loops, ungrounded self-evaluations, and catastrophic planning failures when operating without external formal verification.

## Introduction

The development of artificial intelligence has historically aspired to construct autonomous agents capable of perceiving external environments, deliberating over sequential goals, and enacting changes to achieve desired outcomes. With the advent of foundation models, the autonomous agent paradigm has shifted from domain-specific heuristic systems toward flexible, language-driven architectures. Rather than treating neural models merely as static knowledge bases or passive text generators, modern cognitive frameworks position them as central decision-making controllers.

Understanding the mechanisms that facilitate autonomous agency is critical as these systems are increasingly deployed to execute multi-step software workflows, interact with digital application programming interfaces (APIs), and simulate social dynamics. However, building agents that reliably orchestrate internal cognition alongside external tool use presents formidable challenges, including hallucinations, infinite execution loops, and an absence of formal planning guarantees. This literature review investigates the architectural frameworks designed to resolve these challenges, addressing the specific research question: *What are the main approaches to building AI agents that can reason and act?*

## Methodology

This review evaluates a curated corpus of 15 academic papers addressing autonomous agent architectures, cognitive planning mechanisms, tool integration, and multi-agent coordination. Evidence was identified and extracted via semantic retrieval over segmented passage collections using targeted keyword queries covering single-agent deliberation, tree-search exploration, conversational programming, verbal reinforcement, and empirical planning limitations.

The extracted evidence was subjected to thematic synthesis to identify converging technical methodologies, conceptual frameworks, points of empirical tension, and operational vulnerabilities. A methodological limitation of this approach is its reliance on a predetermined, fixed corpus. While this corpus captures seminal and representative frameworks in foundation-model agency, it does not incorporate the entirety of an exponentially expanding field, nor does it track real-time revisions to empirical benchmarks or closed-source commercial implementations. Consequently, the findings represent a structured qualitative synthesis of core paradigms rather than an exhaustive meta-analysis.

## Findings

### Interleaving Reasoning Traces with External Tool Execution

A primary approach to building actionable agents is the tight coupling of explicit natural language reasoning traces with concrete environmental actions. Historically, autonomous agents have been conceptualized as entities that continuously perceive their environment—whether composed of digital tools, networks, or other systems—and act upon it (Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995). In foundation-model architectures, modern taxonomies categorize this behavior into an integrated loop where reasoning modules, memory recollection, external tool invocation, and environmental feedback interact dynamically (Wang et al., 2023).

Rather than treating reasoning and execution as separate stages, the ReAct paradigm demonstrates that interleaving sparse, versatile internal reasoning traces with discrete task actions significantly enhances performance over action-only baselines (Yao et al., 2023a). Generating reasoning thoughts allows an agent to track sub-goals, process observations, and handle operational exceptions, which simultaneously improves decision-making interpretability and user trust (Yao et al., 2023a). 

This approach is augmented by teaching models to autonomously recognize when their internal parametric knowledge is insufficient. Through self-supervised training on discrete API call tokens, models can learn to invoke specialized external tools—such as calculators or information retrieval systems—to ground their outputs in verifiable computations and facts (Schick et al., 2023). However, this interleaved approach is not without failure modes; models operating in synchronous reasoning-and-acting loops can become trapped in repetitive reasoning errors, redundantly emitting identical thoughts and actions without the internal mechanism to break out of the cycle (Yao et al., 2023a).

### Deliberative Tree-Search and Lookahead Planning

A second major paradigm addresses the limitations of standard linear autoregressive generation through structured search and explicit planning. Standard left-to-right generation and linear reasoning prompts do not incorporate lookahead, exploratory branching, or backtracking mechanisms, which are fundamental to human-like strategic problem-solving (Yao et al., 2023b). 

To resolve this deficiency, deliberate search frameworks such as the Tree of Thoughts (ToT) allow agents to treat intermediate thoughts as discrete nodes within an exploratory tree, enabling heuristic evaluation and systematic search algorithms such as breadth-first or depth-first search (Yao et al., 2023b). Across complex puzzle solving and open-ended text composition, exploring multiple reasoning paths consistently outperforms standard linear prompting and basic input-output generation (Yao et al., 2023b). This corresponds with agent taxonomies that classify multi-path reasoning and external planning components as essential requirements for robust cognitive agency (Wang et al., 2023).

Nonetheless, the efficacy of tree-based planning is fundamentally constrained by the underlying model's simulation capabilities. Empirical investigations reveal that foundation models struggle significantly with genuine autonomous planning in classical domains because their capabilities stem from statistical pattern recognition rather than first-principles simulations over verified world models (Valmeekam et al., 2023). While an unassisted model may propose seemingly plausible plan fragments, it cannot provide formal guarantees of correctness, raising critical questions regarding the safety of relying solely on heuristic self-evaluation during multi-path search (Valmeekam et al., 2023).

### Episodic Verbal Reflection and Structured Memory Streams

Beyond step-by-step reasoning within a single trajectory, an established approach to building agents involves non-parametric learning via natural language reflection and persistent memory architectures. Rather than modifying model parameters through gradient updates, these systems achieve behavioral adaptation through iterative in-context feedback loops (Wang et al., 2023).

The Reflexion architecture demonstrates that agents can convert scalar or binary feedback signals from an environment into natural language critiques across successive task episodes (Shinn et al., 2023). This verbal self-reflection acts as a semantic gradient signal, furnishing the agent with explicit qualitative directions for rectifying past errors in future attempts; this mechanism produces double-digit percentage performance gains across multi-step interactive decision tasks, question answering, and code generation (Shinn et al., 2023). 

Complementing iterative reflection is the deployment of structured, long-term memory architectures. To sustain coherent autonomous behavior over extended timelines, generative agent frameworks utilize comprehensive memory streams that record an agent's experiences, retrieve relevant subsets based on recency, importance, and contextual relevance, and periodically synthesize these logs into higher-level abstract reflections (Park et al., 2023). Memory recollection and planning guided by environmental and human feedback thus constitute a durable architectural alternative to parameter fine-tuning (Wang et al., 2023).

### Multi-Agent Coordination and Conversational Modularization

A fourth approach shifts the locus of reasoning from a single monolithic agent to distributed networks of interacting entities. Rooted in classical multi-agent theory, an agent's operational environment inherently includes collections of other interacting autonomous entities (Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995). Modern agent frameworks adapt this principle by organizing multi-step workflows around structured inter-agent dialogues (Wang et al., 2023).

The AutoGen framework formalizes this multi-agent paradigm under the concept of "conversation programming," which structures application development by defining sets of specialized, conversable agents that cooperatively resolve complex workflows through structured messaging (Wu et al., 2023). By assigning distinct personas, specialized tool access, and specific sub-tasks to individual agents, conversation programming decomposes challenging end-to-end tasks into modular conversational exchanges (Wu et al., 2023). This division of labor provides an alternative to managing complex, multi-objective context histories within a single model instance.

## Discussion

The synthesized literature demonstrates clear areas of consensus regarding the foundational requirements of agent design. Across classical theory and modern implementations, there is broad agreement that intelligent agency requires a situated perception-action loop rather than ungrounded, single-pass generation (Wooldridge & Jennings, 1995; Wang et al., 2023). Empirical findings consistently demonstrate that direct, unguided generation is inadequate for complex reasoning tasks, whereas explicitly structuring deliberation—whether through interleaved traces (Yao et al., 2023a) or tree search (Yao et al., 2023b)—yields measurable performance advantages. Furthermore, the literature converges on in-context adaptation (via verbal critiques and memory streams) as a preferred alternative to gradient-based parameter updates for behavioral self-correction (Park et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023).

However, significant conceptual and architectural tensions exist within the field:

1. **Pattern Matching versus Rigorous World Models:** A profound division exists between research demonstrating the empirical utility of heuristic tree search (Yao et al., 2023b) and work critically exposing the planning limitations of language models (Valmeekam et al., 2023). While models can generate search spaces and evaluate candidate thoughts heuristics-wise, they fundamentally lack underlying world-model simulations, meaning their plans lack the formal correctness guarantees that dedicated domain planners provide (Valmeekam et al., 2023). A plausible implication of this tension is that heuristic search methods risk efficiently exploring hallucinated paths unless tied to external deterministic verifiers.
2. **Synchronous Interleaving versus Asynchronous Episodic Reflection:** A technical divergence exists between the operational timing of error recovery. Frameworks like ReAct enforce synchronous, step-by-step reasoning interleaved with action to catch and adapt to errors dynamically during execution (Yao et al., 2023a). In contrast, frameworks like Reflexion operate asynchronously across episodes, allowing full trajectory execution to fail before distilling verbal reflections to guide the subsequent trial (Shinn et al., 2023). This tension reflects divergent assumptions regarding environmental cost: step-level feedback is essential when action execution carries heavy immediate penalties, whereas episodic reflection suits environments where task success can only be determined upon terminal evaluation.
3. **Monolithic Search versus Social Modularization:** A strategic divergence divides internal algorithmic expansion and multi-agent decomposition. While Tree of Thoughts addresses reasoning complexity by expanding internal search trees within a single agent (Yao et al., 2023b), multi-agent frameworks distribute complexity outward across conversable, role-specialized entities (Wu et al., 2023). 

A critical gap across the corpus is the lack of systematic accounting for the operational overhead of these advanced architectures. While tree search and multi-agent conversations enhance performance, the evidence does not quantify the exponential growth in token consumption and execution latency; a plausible implication is that the high computational cost of recursive branching and multi-turn dialogue limits their economic viability for real-time applications. Furthermore, while execution loops represent an acknowledged failure mode (Yao et al., 2023a), the corpus lacks robust frameworks for execution sandboxing, deterministic loop-breaking, or fail-safe operational guardrails.

These findings suggest that the practical role of foundation models in agent design is transitioning from that of an isolated, end-to-end thinker to that of a semantic orchestrator. Because language models excel at natural language parsing, tool invocation, and role coordination (Schick et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023) but falter at first-principles logical planning (Valmeekam et al., 2023), robust autonomous architectures increasingly rely on the model to mediate between human intent, structured memory, and external deterministic execution environments.

## Conclusion

The main approaches to building AI agents that can reason and act encompass four primary paradigms: interleaved reasoning and tool execution (ReAct, Toolformer), deliberative search and lookahead planning (Tree of Thoughts), non-parametric episodic reflection and memory streaming (Reflexion, Generative Agents), and multi-agent conversational modularization (AutoGen). 

Despite significant empirical gains across reasoning benchmarks, foundational open problems remain. Chief among these are resolving the self-evaluation paradox—wherein an ungrounded model must reliably evaluate its own generated reasoning branches—mitigating persistent degenerative action loops, and bridging the divide between heuristic linguistic pattern matching and rigorous, verified world-model planning. Future agent design must focus on formalizing hybrid architectures that couple flexible language-based orchestration with deterministic, external verification mechanisms.
